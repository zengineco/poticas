Potica in Minnesota

By Poticas.com  ·  Updated August 2026

Minnesota’s Iron Range may be the most potica-soaked ground in America — where Slovenian miners turned a Slovenian celebration bread into regional identity food that everyone bakes, and one of the famous Three P’s.

Why is potica so popular in Minnesota?

Slovenian and Croatian miners settled Minnesota’s Iron Range in huge numbers from the 1880s onward, and potica embedded so deeply that Minnesotans of every ancestry now bake it. On the Range it is one of the "Three P’s" — potica, pasties, and porketta — the shared table of Slovenian, Cornish, and Italian mining families. It is regional identity food, not just heritage.

The Iron Range’s Bread

When Slovenian and Croatian miners came to work the iron of northern Minnesota between the 1880s and the 1930s, they brought potica with them — and on the Mesabi and Vermilion ranges it put down roots as deep as anywhere in America. Range towns like Hibbing, Chisholm, Virginia, Eveleth, and Ely filled with Slovenian families, their national homes, and their kitchens. Potica was the bread of celebration: Christmas, Easter, weddings, funerals, and any occasion that called for something better than daily bread.

What makes the Range unusual is how far the bread traveled beyond its own community. In most of America potica stayed an ethnic heirloom; on the Iron Range it became regional identity food that Minnesotans of every ancestry bake, buy, and argue about.

The Three P’s

Ask anyone from the Range about its food and you will hear the Three P’s: potica, pasties, and porketta — the celebration bread of the Slovenians, the hand pie of the Cornish, and the garlicky pork roast of the Italians. Three immigrant miners’ foods that crossed every ethnic boundary underground and at the table.

Potica is the sweet one, the holiday one, the one carried to the neighbors. That a Slovenian walnut roll became one-third of a whole region’s shared cuisine says everything about how deep it set in Minnesota.

The Minnesota Style

Potica changed remarkably little in America — the ingredients were all on the shelf, and diaspora bakers guarded the recipe as an heirloom rather than adapting it. The one signature the Iron Range added is a matter of degree: Range bakers push the dough even thinner and the filling ratio even higher, so a Minnesota potica slice can look almost more walnut than bread.

Walnut is the classic filling here, exactly as it is in the old country — the same walnut potica that carries the EU-protected Slovenian name. If you want to bake the Range version, roll it thin and don’t be shy with the filling.

Where to Buy Potica in Minnesota

The Range still bakes it commercially. Sunrise Bakery in Hibbing has hand-rolled potica since 1913 and ships nationwide — the closest thing to a Range institution that will mail you one. Farther south, Tobies in Hinckley bakes the award-winning Jan’s Potica, and Twin Cities cottage bakers still make it to order. Around Virginia and Hibbing, church groups and community sales remain a quiet holiday source that never shows up online.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is potica so popular in Minnesota?

Slovenian and Croatian miners settled the Iron Range in huge numbers from the 1880s onward, and potica embedded so deeply that Minnesotans of every ancestry bake it. On the Range it is one of the Three P’s, the region’s shared celebration food — regional identity, not just ethnic heritage.

Where can I buy potica in Minnesota?

Around Virginia and Hibbing, church and community holiday sales are a quiet local source. Our where-to-buy census lists the verified sellers.

What are the Three P’s of the Iron Range?

Potica, pasties, and porketta — the Slovenian celebration bread, the Cornish hand pie, and the Italian garlic-pork roast. Three immigrant miners’ foods that crossed every ethnic line and became the Iron Range’s shared table.

What Minnesota towns are known for potica?

Hibbing, Chisholm, Virginia, Eveleth, and Ely — the Mesabi and Vermilion range towns that drew large Slovenian and Croatian populations. Hibbing, home of Sunrise Bakery, is the best known for it.

Is potica a Minnesota food or a Slovenian one?

Both, honestly. It is Slovenian in origin — an EU-protected Slovenian specialty — but Minnesota’s Iron Range is one of its great American homes, where it long ago stopped being only an ethnic dish and became regional identity food baked by families of every ancestry.
