Tarragon Potica: Pehtranova Potica

By Poticas.com  ·  Updated July 2026

The one that surprises everyone — a sweet herb roll with no equivalent anywhere else in European pastry. Slovenians consider it among the most traditional of all.

The One That Surprises Everyone

Most people outside Slovenia have never met a sweet herb pastry, and on paper the combination reads like a mistake. It is not. Tarragon — pehtran in Slovene — is one of the five fillings approved for the EU-protected "Slovenska potica", alongside walnut, walnut-raisin, poppy seed, and cottage cheese. In Slovenia, tarragon lives in the sweet kitchen: fresh, anise-scented, folded into sugar and butter and cream, it produces a flavour with no equivalent anywhere else in European baking.

An Easter Tradition

Pehtranova potica — affectionately pehtranka — is a spring and Easter bake. Fresh tarragon comes up early, right on time for the Easter table, and the pale-green roll sits beside the ham and coloured eggs in many Slovenian households. It is a specialty especially cherished in the Dolenjska region around Novo mesto, where local bakers treat it as a point of pride, though you will find it across the country.

The Filling

Two traditional styles exist, and both are legitimate:

  • Butter-cream style: softened butter creamed with sugar, eggs, and sour cream, with finely chopped blanched tarragon folded through.
  • Cottage-cheese style (s skuto): the tarragon folded into a sweetened curd/cottage-cheese filling — combining two of the five protected fillings in one roll. Common, beloved, and slightly moister.

For the butter-cream version, per one loaf of the standard dough:

  • 80–100 g fresh tarragon leaves, stripped from their stems
  • 150 g sugar
  • 80 g butter, softened
  • 2 eggs
  • 100 ml sour cream
  • Pinch of salt

Method Notes

Blanch the tarragon leaves for 30 seconds in boiling water and plunge them straight into ice water — this sets the colour and softens the herb's raw edge. Squeeze dry and chop very fine, almost to a paste. Cream the butter and sugar, beat in the eggs one at a time, add the sour cream, fold in the tarragon, season with a pinch of salt.

The filling goes on pale green and looks improbable. Bake it anyway. Roll, prove, and bake exactly as for the walnut version — 175 °C, 50 minutes, rolled thin and cooled fully. The green softens to olive in the oven and the whole house smells like a Slovenian village kitchen in April.

This is not a first-timer's potica. Learn the dough and the roll on walnut; come back to tarragon when your hands know the motions.

Fresh Tarragon Only

Dried tarragon produces a different and significantly lesser thing — dusty where it should be bright. If you cannot find fresh tarragon (look for French tarragon; buy generously, the leaves cook down), wait until you can. The herb is the pastry.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does tarragon potica taste like?

Sweet first, then a gentle anise-like warmth from the fresh tarragon — closer to a fennel-scented cream than anything savory. It is unusual on the first bite and addictive by the third. Slovenians consider it a classic, not a novelty.

Can I use dried tarragon for pehtranova potica?

No — dried tarragon loses the bright anise aromatics that define the pastry and leaves only a dusty, faintly medicinal note. Fresh French tarragon is the ingredient. If you can't get it, bake a different filling and wait for spring.

Is tarragon potica traditional or a modern invention?

Fully traditional — tarragon is one of the five fillings approved in the EU "Slovenska potica" specification, and pehtranova potica is a long-standing Easter bake, especially cherished around Novo mesto in the Dolenjska region.

What is pehtranova potica s skuto?

The cottage-cheese variant: sweetened curd (skuta) filling with the chopped tarragon folded through. It combines two of the five protected fillings and bakes slightly moister than the butter-cream style.
