Apricot Potica

By Poticas.com  ·  Updated August 2026

The bright one. Where walnut is rich and deep, apricot is tart and sunny — a fruit filling that gives the roll a lift. Common across the Central European nut-roll family, and easy to love.

What is apricot potica?

A potica filled with a thick, tangy apricot paste — made from dried apricots simmered soft and pureed, or from a good apricot jam — sometimes blended with ground walnuts or almonds for body. It is a fruit variant rather than one of the protected traditional fillings, and it is popular across the wider Central European nut-roll family, from Slovenian kitchens to Hungarian bejgli.

The Bright One

Most poticas are built on richness — walnut, cheese, honey. Apricot goes the other way. Its filling is tart and bright, and that acidity cuts the buttery dough in a way the nut versions never do. It is the roll to make when you want something lighter on the tongue.

Be clear about its place: apricot is a fruit variant, not one of the five fillings protected under the EU “Slovenska potica” name. But fruit-filled rolls are everywhere in the Central European family this pastry belongs to — apricot is a classic filling for Hungarian bejgli, for one — so it is well within the tradition, just not the protected core.

The Apricot Filling

The key is a thick paste. Watery fruit is what ruins a fruit potica. Per one loaf of the standard dough:

  • 250 g dried apricots
  • 60–80 g sugar, to taste (dried apricots are already sweet-tart)
  • Juice of half a lemon
  • Optional: 80 g ground walnuts or almonds, for body

Method: simmer the dried apricots in just enough water to cover until very soft, about 15–20 minutes, then drain and puree with the sugar and lemon. Cook the puree down further if it is loose — it should mound on a spoon, not slide off. Fold in the ground nuts if you want a filling with more grip. A good thick apricot jam can stand in for the whole thing in a pinch; warm it and stir in a little ground nut to firm it up.

Why Fruit Fillings Need Care

Fruit fillings carry more free moisture than nut fillings, and thin potica dough is unforgiving of it. Two rules keep the roll from going soggy:

  • Reduce the paste thick. If you can see it weeping liquid, it is too wet — cook it down more and let it cool before spreading.
  • Spread thin and do not overfill. A modest even layer bakes through; a heavy wet one steams the dough from the inside.

Rolling and Baking

Once the paste is thick and cool, roll and bake exactly as for the classic walnut potica — rolled thin, about 175 °C for 50 minutes, cooled fully before slicing. The apricot layer sets as it cools, so a warm roll that looks loose will firm up on the rack.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is apricot potica traditional?

It is a fruit variant rather than one of the five fillings protected under the EU Slovenska potica name. That said, fruit-filled rolls are common across the Central European family potica belongs to — apricot is a classic bejgli filling in Hungary — so it is well within the broader tradition.

Can I use apricot jam instead of dried apricots?

Yes. A thick, good-quality apricot jam works as a shortcut. Warm it so it spreads, and stir in a spoonful of ground walnut or almond to firm it up, since jam alone can be too loose and sweet for a clean slice.

How do I keep an apricot potica from going soggy?

Cook the filling down until it is thick enough to mound on a spoon, let it cool before spreading, and use a modest even layer. Free moisture is what soaks thin potica dough, so a thick, cool paste is the whole trick.

Does apricot potica go well with nuts?

Yes — an apricot-walnut or apricot-almond blend is a common and excellent version. The ground nut gives the tangy fruit some body and grip, and the two flavors balance the sweetness.
