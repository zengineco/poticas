Grinding Walnuts: Texture Is Everything

By Poticas.com  ·  Updated July 2026

Half a kilo of walnuts goes into one potica. How you grind them decides whether the filling is silk or sludge.

How do you grind walnuts for potica?

Toast the walnuts eight minutes at 160 °C, cool them completely, then grind to the texture of coarse damp sand — a hand-crank nut mill is ideal, or a food processor used in small batches with one-second pulses. Grinding warm nuts, or over-grinding, releases the oil and turns the filling into bitter paste.

The Texture Target

You are aiming for coarse, slightly damp sand — every piece small enough to spread smoothly, none of it packed into paste. Too coarse and the filling bakes loose and crumbly, falling out of every slice. Too fine — past sand into butter — and the walnuts have released their oil: the filling turns greasy and heavy, and over-worked walnut oil reads bitter.

Rub a pinch between your fingers. Sand that barely clumps: correct. A ball that holds together and shines: you have started making walnut butter — stop.

Toast First, Always

Eight minutes at 160 °C, spread in one layer, then cooled completely before grinding. Toasting deepens the flavour dramatically — raw walnut is flat and faintly astringent next to toasted. The cooling is not optional: warm nuts are soft nuts, and soft nuts smear into paste the moment a blade touches them. Cold, brittle nuts shatter cleanly into sand.

Food Processor Technique

  • Work in batches — no more than 150 g at a time. A full bowl grinds the bottom to paste while the top stays whole.
  • Pulse, never run. One-second pulses, checking between them. Continuous running builds heat, and heat releases oil.
  • Add a spoonful of the recipe's sugar to each batch. Sugar absorbs the surface oil and keeps the grind free-flowing — an old trick that works.
  • Stop early. The step from "almost there" to "walnut butter" is about three pulses. You can always pulse again; you cannot un-paste.

Buying & Storing Walnuts

Buy pieces, not halves — you are grinding them anyway, and pieces are meaningfully cheaper. You need 500 g shelled for one loaf, so buy in bulk if potica is becoming a habit.

Taste before you commit. Walnut oil goes rancid quietly; one stale nut ruins half a kilo of filling. Fresh walnut tastes sweet and mild. Bitter, paint-like, or musty means rancid — discard the batch.

Store in the freezer. Shelled walnuts keep only about a month at cool room temperature but a year frozen, in an airtight bag. Grind from frozen or near-frozen — cold nuts grind better anyway (see toasting note above; toast, cool, then grind).

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use pre-ground walnut meal for potica?

In a pinch, yes, but check freshness aggressively — pre-ground walnut goes rancid much faster than whole pieces because more surface is exposed. And you lose the option to toast before grinding, which costs real flavour.

Why did my walnut filling turn oily and bitter?

The walnuts were over-ground (usually a food processor left running instead of pulsed) or ground while still warm from toasting. Both release walnut oil, which reads greasy and slightly bitter after baking.

How many walnuts do I need for one potica?

The classic recipe uses 500 g of shelled walnuts for one large loaf — roughly equal weight to the flour. That ratio is what makes potica potica.

Do walnuts really need toasting for potica?

The filling works without it, but toasting (8 minutes at 160 °C, cooled fully) deepens the flavour considerably and dries the nut so it grinds cleaner. Skipping it is the single easiest way to make a flat-tasting potica.
