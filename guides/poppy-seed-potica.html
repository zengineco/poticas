Poppy Seed Potica: Makova Potica

By Poticas.com  ·  Updated July 2026

Darker, more fragrant, and arguably the most striking of all the variations — almost black inside, flecked with orange zest.

What is poppy seed potica (makova potica)?

The dark, fragrant sibling of the walnut roll: ground poppy seeds mixed with sugar, hot milk, butter, and orange zest, rolled inside the same enriched yeast dough. Called makovnjača in Croatia, where it anchors the Christmas table. The seeds must be ground — whole poppy seeds bake up gritty and nearly flavourless.

The Dark Sibling

Slice a walnut potica and a poppy seed potica side by side and you get the two poles of Central European baking: gold spirals against near-black ones. The poppy filling is darker in flavour too — gently bitter, floral, deeply fragrant in a way walnut never quite reaches. Makova potica is among Slovenia's most traditional varieties — though note the narrow list protected by the 2021 EU "Slovenska potica" specification runs to walnut and tarragon fillings; poppy's kingdom is the broader tradition, and above all Croatia's Christmas table. Its partisans are the most loyal in the entire potica world.

Whose Roll Is It?

Everyone's. In Slovenia, makova potica shares the holiday table with walnut. In Croatia the same roll is makovnjača — a fixture of Christmas, and in Slavonia a table without it is considered incomplete. Hungary bakes the poppy bejgli at Christmas; Poland has makowiec. The poppy branch of the rolled-pastry family tree may be its most international one (the family tree, mapped).

You Must Grind the Seeds

The step everyone skips, and the reason so many home poppy rolls disappoint: whole poppy seeds are sealed capsules. Baked whole, they stay gritty and taste like almost nothing. Ground, they release their oils and become the dark, fragrant paste the pastry is named for.

Grind in a dedicated poppy/spice grinder, a clean burr coffee grinder, or a high-speed blender in short bursts, until the seeds look like coarse wet sand and clump slightly. (A food processor alone is usually too coarse for poppy — the seeds are tiny and slip the blade.) If you can only find pre-ground poppy, buy it from a busy Eastern European grocer and use it fast: ground poppy goes rancid quickly.

The Filling

Per one loaf of the standard dough:

  • 350 g poppy seeds, ground (see above)
  • 120 g sugar
  • 80 ml hot milk
  • 40 g butter, melted
  • 1 tsp vanilla
  • Zest of one orange
  • 2 tbsp rum (optional, but traditional)

Pour the hot milk and melted butter over the ground seeds, add everything else, and stir to a thick, spreadable paste. Too loose, add more ground seed; too stiff, a splash more warm milk. The orange zest is the signature — those bright flecks against the near-black filling.

Method Notes

Everything else follows the walnut playbook: roll thin, spread generously (poppy filling cooks down more than walnut — do not be shy), roll tight, seal, prove 45 minutes, bake at 175 °C for about 50 minutes, and cool completely before slicing. Slice thin: poppy potica is intense, and a slim dark spiral next to a gold walnut one is the best-looking board in Central European baking.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I really have to grind poppy seeds for potica?

Yes. Whole poppy seeds are hard little capsules that bake up gritty and bland. Grinding ruptures them and releases the oils — that is where all the flavour lives. A spice grinder, burr grinder, or blender in bursts gets them to the coarse-wet-sand stage you need.

What is the difference between makova potica and makovnjača?

The same poppy seed roll wearing its Slovenian name (makova potica) and its Croatian name (makovnjača). Croatia leans on it at Christmas; Slovenia bakes it for holidays alongside the walnut version.

Why is my poppy seed filling bitter?

Either the seeds were stale (poppy goes rancid fast, especially pre-ground — buy fresh, store cold) or the filling lacks the sugar, milk, and zest that round poppy's natural gentle bitterness into fragrance.

Can I use canned poppy seed filling?

It works mechanically, but canned filling is mostly sugar syrup and runs sweeter, looser, and flatter than fresh-ground seeds with hot milk and orange zest. For the roll the pastry is named after, grind fresh.
