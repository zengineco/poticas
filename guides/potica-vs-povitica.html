Potica vs. Povitica vs. Nut Roll: What's the Difference?

By Poticas.com  ·  Updated July 2026

Mostly the same pastry, wearing different passports. Which word a family uses tells you where they came from.

The Short Answer

Potica and povitica are the same pastry with two different names. Both descend from the Slovenian verb poviti — to wrap, to roll up. The older form povitica survived in Croatian and Serbian communities; in Slovenia the word contracted over time to potica. The American "nut roll" is the plain-English label for the same family of rolled, filled yeast breads. Babka and strudel are different pastries — more on those below.

One Verb, Many Names

The name is the instruction: poviti, to wrap. Everything the pastry is — the thin dough, the filling spread edge to edge, the tight spiral — lives inside that verb. As the pastry standardised into Slovenia's national dish, the four-syllable povitica wore down to three: potica. The full etymology, with the regional name table (Austrian Potize, Triestine putizza, Croatian orehnjača), is in our About Potica reference.

Who Says Which

"Potica" is what you hear in Slovenia and in Slovenian-American communities — Cleveland, Pittsburgh, Indianapolis, and Minnesota's Iron Range, where bakeries like Hibbing's Sunrise Bakery (founded 1913) have kept it a keystone product for over a century.

"Povitica" survives in Croatian and Serbian communities that never shortened the word. The famous example is Kansas City's Strawberry Hill neighbourhood, settled by Croatian immigrants in the early 1900s. The family behind the Strawberry Hill Baking Company turned their grandmothers' holiday povitica into a business in 1984, and a fourth generation of the family bakes it today in Merriam, Kansas. In that community the word is povitica, full stop — the name marks identity.

"Nut roll" is the catch-all American term, common in Pennsylvania and the broader Rust Belt, covering potica, povitica, Hungarian bejgli, Polish makowiec, and Slovak orechovník — overlapping cousins from the same Central European family.

In cities where Slovenians and Croatians settled side by side — Cleveland, Pittsburgh — you will hear both words. Families know which one is theirs, and will usually tell you the other one is slightly wrong.

What Is NOT the Same Pastry

Strudel

Strudel dough is unleavened — flour, water, oil — stretched to transparency and baked crisp and flaky. Potica dough is an enriched yeast dough that proves and rises, baking to a soft, brioche-like crumb. Strudel is mostly filling in a thin wrapper; potica is near-equal partners of bread and filling. Strudel is best warm the day it is baked; potica genuinely improves overnight (see storing).

Babka

Babka starts similarly — enriched dough, spread filling, rolled — but then the roll is split and twisted, producing open, marbled layers rather than potica's closed concentric spirals. Babka also carries much less filling per bite. Related family, different member.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are potica and povitica the same thing?

Yes — the same rolled, filled yeast bread under two forms of the same Slovenian word (from poviti, "to wrap"). Povitica is the older form kept by Croatian and Serbian communities; potica is the contracted Slovenian standard.

Is potica just a nut roll?

"Nut roll" is the umbrella American term, and potica is the Slovenian member of that family. If someone from Cleveland or the Iron Range hands you a nut roll, there is a fair chance it is a potica by ancestry.

Is potica the same as babka?

No. Both start as rolled enriched dough, but babka is split and twisted after rolling and carries far less filling. Potica keeps its closed spiral and a near 1:1 filling-to-dough ratio.

Why does Kansas City say povitica instead of potica?

Kansas City's Strawberry Hill neighbourhood was settled by Croatian immigrants, and Croatian communities kept the older form of the word. The Strawberry Hill Baking Company has made povitica commercially since 1984, now in its fourth family generation.
