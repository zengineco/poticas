Potica in America

By Poticas.com  ·  Updated July 2026

It arrived in steamer trunks and in the hands of grandmothers who had no written recipe. A century later, a senator serves it in Washington.

How did potica come to America?

Slovene and Croatian immigrants carried the recipe between the 1880s and 1930s to the industrial Midwest — Cleveland, Pittsburgh, Kansas City, and Minnesota's Iron Range, where potica became one of the region's "Three P's" alongside pasties and porketta. Bakeries in Hibbing and Kansas City still hand-roll it today.

The Crossing

Between roughly 1880 and 1930, hundreds of thousands of Slovenians and Croatians emigrated to industrial America — the mines, the steel mills, the packing houses. They settled in tight communities: Cleveland, Pittsburgh, Chicago, Milwaukee, Pueblo, Kansas City, and the iron towns of northern Minnesota and Michigan. The potica recipe travelled with them, mostly unwritten — carried in the hands of women who had learned it by standing next to their own mothers.

Unlike many immigrant dishes, potica barely changed in America. Flour, butter, eggs, walnuts, honey — nothing needed substituting, and nobody wanted to modernise it. This was the thing from home. You preserved it.

The Iron Range & the Three P's

On Minnesota's Iron Range — Hibbing, Ely, Eveleth, Chisholm — potica did something remarkable: it stopped being ethnic food and became regional food. Rangers talk about the Three P's that define the local table across all ancestries: potica (Slovenian), pasties (Cornish), and porketta (Italian) — three immigrant miners' foods that crossed every ethnic line because they were simply that good.

The Range is also home to one of America's great potica institutions: Sunrise Bakery in Hibbing, founded in 1913 by Giulio Forti, a scratch baker from Rome. Potica entered the family in 1932, when Forti's son Vincent married the daughter of Croatian immigrants and she taught him the craft — the Range's intermarried food history in a single wedding. The bakery put potica on its commercial roster around 1960, and a 1991 Los Angeles Times mention set its mail-order phones ringing; hand-rolled loaves still ship nationwide to homesick Rangers.

Iron Range potica has its own reputation: dough stretched past thin toward transparent — the newspaper-test tradition at full strength — with the filling ratio pushed to the maximum.

Cleveland & the Slovenian Cities

Cleveland held one of the largest Slovenian communities outside Slovenia, centred on the St. Clair Avenue corridor, and it became a preservation zone for Slovenian food culture — bakeries, church sales, and cultural societies where potica marked every Easter, Christmas, wedding, and funeral. The same pattern repeated in Pittsburgh, Milwaukee, Joliet, and Pueblo. In Kansas City, the Croatian version of the story produced Strawberry Hill's povitica — same pastry, older name.

The unwritten rule in all of these communities: you don't bring store-bought. If you brought a potica, you made it. Nobody ever wrote that rule down. Everyone knows it. (The full map of the nut-roll belt — every city, every name — lives in the Traditional Nut Roll field guide.)

The Cookbooks That Saved It

A recipe transmitted by demonstration has a weakness: the third and fourth generation, who were never in the kitchen when it happened. The diaspora saw the break coming and responded with survival documents.

The landmark is Woman's Glory — The Kitchen, published by the Slovenian Women's Union of America for its silver anniversary (1926–1951): 275 pages assembled explicitly, in editor Albina Novak's words, to serve as an "instructor" for women who wished to make "dishes for which their mothers have always been famous" — written for the reader with no living grandmother left to ask. The Union's magazine Zarja — The Dawn has carried recipes and kitchen tradition to Slovenian-American homes since the late 1920s.

Those books, plus a thousand church and lodge cookbooks across the Midwest, are why the recipe survived the century. If your family's version lives only in someone's hands, write it down. That is literally why this site's community exists.

Potica in Washington

The pastry's American story now reaches the Capitol: Senator Amy Klobuchar of Minnesota — Slovenian on her father's Iron Range side — has famously served potica to colleagues in Washington, D.C., where it reportedly won fans on both sides of the aisle. It is a quiet, precise statement: this is where I come from, and this is what my people have been baking in the mining towns for over a century.

From a steamer trunk to the Senate. The recipe travelled. It survived. It is still here — and if you want to make the version those miners' grandmothers made, start with the walnut potica.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the Three P's of the Iron Range?

Potica, pasties, and porketta — Slovenian, Cornish, and Italian miners' foods that crossed every ethnic boundary on Minnesota's Iron Range and became the region's shared table. Potica is the celebration bread of the three.

Where can I buy traditional potica in the United States?

Iron Range and Cleveland-area bakeries still hand-roll it — Sunrise Bakery in Hibbing (since 1913) ships nationwide, and Kansas City's Strawberry Hill Baking Company ships the Croatian povitica. The shop page lists trusted sources.

Why is potica so popular in Minnesota?

Slovenian and Croatian miners settled Minnesota's Iron Range in huge numbers from the 1880s onward, and potica embedded so deeply that locals of every ancestry bake it. It is now regional identity food, not just ethnic heritage.

Did potica change when it came to America?

Remarkably little. Its ingredients were all available in American groceries, and diaspora bakers treated the recipe as an heirloom to preserve rather than adapt. The one American signature: Iron Range bakers push the dough even thinner and the filling ratio even higher.
