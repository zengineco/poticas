Potica Troubleshooting: Every Failure Has a Cause

By Poticas.com  ·  Updated July 2026

Cut the loaf, read the cross-section. The spiral tells you exactly what went wrong — and none of it is fatal.

Why did my potica crack or have gaps inside?

Gaps between the layers come from a loose roll, an over-proved second rise, or a filling too dry to bond to the dough. A cracked top means the tin was too small, the oven too hot, or the surface dried during proving. A doughy centre means underbaked — potica is done at about 90 °C internal.

Gaps Between the Layers

The classic potica failure: you slice, and there are hollow tunnels where filling should meet dough. Three causes, in order of likelihood:

  • The roll was loose. Air trapped between turns expands in the oven, then the structure collapses around it. Roll firmly — the cloth technique makes a tight roll much easier.
  • Over-proved in the tin. During the second prove the dough inflates away from the filling; in the oven that exaggerated rise collapses into a gap, usually a crescent under the top crust. Keep the second prove to about 45 minutes.
  • Filling too dry. A dry filling doesn't adhere to the dough, so the layers separate where they never bonded. Respect the recipe's honey, rum, and egg white — they are the glue.

Cracked Top

A crack means internal pressure found a weak point. Either the tin was too small (the loaf had nowhere to grow but up and out), the oven ran hot (crust set before the inside finished expanding), or the surface dried into a rigid skin during the second prove — cover the tin loosely with a cloth while it proves. A hairline crack is cosmetic; a canyon is a message.

Filling Leaked Out

Caramelised filling welded to the tin means either the filling was too liquid or the seam let go. Honey heated too far is the usual culprit for runny filling — warm it just to pourable. For the seam: leave the 4 cm bare strip when spreading, pinch the seam shut along its entire length, and bake seam-down (or seam-outward in a ring mould) so gravity works with you.

Dense or Doughy Centre

Potica is dense by design, but it should never be gummy or raw at the core. Real causes:

  • Underbaked. This loaf is a heat-sink. Trust a thermometer (about 90 °C in the deepest point) or a skewer, not the surface colour — the top browns long before the middle cooks. Loaf tins are more prone to this than ring moulds; the cone exists precisely for this reason.
  • Under-proved dough bakes dense everywhere, not just centrally. The poke test prevents it.
  • Sliced warm. A warm potica reads gummy even when perfectly baked. The crumb sets as it cools. Full cooling is part of the bake.

Dry Potica

Overbaking is the obvious cause — tent with foil once the top is golden, and pull it at temperature rather than by the clock. The subtler cause is a mean filling: the ratio of nuts to honey/rum/egg-white exists to keep the interior moist for days. A skimped filling bakes into sawdust no matter how good the dough is. And note that potica dries from the cut face once opened — store it cut-side covered.

The Consolation

A cracked, gapped, lopsided potica still tastes like potica. The filling does the work; the spiral is more forgiving than it looks. Diagnose, take the note for next time, and eat the evidence. If you want a second opinion on the cross-section, post it in the community — potica autopsies welcome.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did my potica separate from the filling?

Either the roll was loose (trapped air expands, then collapses into gaps), the shaped loaf over-proved in the tin (dough inflates away from the spiral), or the filling was too dry to bond to the dough. Tight roll, 45-minute second prove, full-moisture filling.

How do I know when potica is fully baked?

Internal temperature around 90 °C at the deepest point, or a skewer that comes out with no wet dough. Surface colour is misleading — the top browns long before the dense centre finishes.

Why is my potica gummy in the middle?

Underbaked, under-proved, or — most commonly — sliced while still warm. The crumb of an enriched loaf sets during cooling; even a perfect potica reads gummy if you cut it hot.

Can I fix a potica that cracked on top?

Not after the fact — but it is purely cosmetic. Next bake: larger tin, verify the oven temperature, and cover the loaf loosely during the second prove so the surface never dries into a rigid skin.
