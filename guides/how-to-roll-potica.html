How to Roll Potica Dough

By Poticas.com  ·  Updated July 2026

The dough is not the hard part. The roll is the hard part — and it is entirely learnable.

How thin should potica dough be rolled?

About 3 mm for a home bake — thin enough to almost see your hand through it. The traditional benchmark is thinner still: dough you can read a newspaper through. Roll from the centre outward on a floured cloth, and rest the dough five minutes whenever it springs back.

Prove It Properly First

Under-proved dough tears. It has not developed enough elasticity to stretch thin without snapping back or splitting. Your dough should have visibly doubled, feel light and slightly tacky, and spring back slowly when you poke it. Springs back instantly? Give it more time. Doesn't spring back at all? Slightly over-proved — still usable, just work quickly. (Full proving guide here.)

The Surface

Do not roll on a bare counter. Flour the surface generously, lay a clean cotton kitchen cloth over it, and flour the cloth. The cloth does two jobs: it stops the dough sticking, and at the end it lifts the dough into the roll for you, so your hands never have to drag on thin dough. This cloth technique is the traditional method — diaspora accounts describe families spreading sheets over entire tables, and even beds, to get enough working surface.

  • Best: floured cotton cloth over any large flat surface; marble underneath is a bonus (stays cool, resists sticking)
  • Fine: a large silicone baking mat, well floured
  • Avoid: bare plastic — it grips thin dough and creates suction

The Rolling Technique

Start in the centre and roll outward in all directions — not back and forth, which tightens the gluten you are trying to relax. Work gently and evenly.

When the dough starts fighting you — springing back after every pass — stop. Cover it with the cloth and walk away for five minutes. The gluten relaxes and the dough rolls out easily again. This single trick prevents most tearing, because tearing happens when you force dough that wanted a rest.

How Thin? The Newspaper Test

For a first potica, aim for about 3 mm everywhere — hold it up to the light and you should almost see your hand through it. Thicker dough gives you a bready loaf with two fat spirals; thinner dough gives you the proper filling-to-dough ratio in every bite. The EU specification for "Slovenska potica" requires at least three to four full turns of dough and filling in the finished loaf, and that is only possible with thin dough.

The traditional benchmark goes further. In potica and povitica families — Slovenia, Croatia, and the American diaspora alike — the folk standard is dough thin enough to read a newspaper through. Bakers on Minnesota's Iron Range and in Kansas City's Croatian community still describe it exactly that way: hold the stretched dough up, and if you can make out the print, you are ready to fill. That level is a lifetime skill; do not demand it of yourself on attempt one.

Spreading the Filling

Spread to within 2 cm of the edge on three sides. On the far long edge leave 4 cm bare — that is where the seam seals, and it needs clean dough. Spread more thickly than feels right; the filling compresses as you roll. Thin, mean filling is how you end up with a bread loaf that has a rumour of walnut in it.

The Roll Itself

Lift the near edge of the cloth to start the roll — let the cloth flip the first turn so you never pinch thin dough with your fingers. Once it is moving, guide it from underneath with flat hands. Roll firmly and evenly: looseness now becomes air pockets later, and air pockets collapse in the oven into hollow gaps between layers.

Seal the seam by pressing and pinching the bare edge along its full length. Tuck the ends under. Into the greased tin seam-down.

If It Tears

It happens to everyone. Press the tear closed with wet fingers and keep going — a mid-roll tear disappears inside the loaf. The only tear that matters is at the very end near the seam; patch that one carefully. A patched potica bakes exactly like an unpatched one.

Your first potica will not be perfect. It will still be the best thing you bake that year.

Frequently Asked Questions

How thin should potica dough be rolled?

About 3 mm for a home bake — you should almost see your hand through it against the light. The traditional benchmark is thinner still: dough you can read a newspaper through. The EU "Slovenska potica" specification requires at least 3–4 full turns of dough and filling per loaf, which demands thin dough.

Why does my potica dough keep springing back?

The gluten is tight. Cover the dough and rest it for five minutes, then continue — it will roll out easily. Forcing it instead is the main cause of tearing.

What do I do if the dough tears while rolling?

Press the tear together with wet fingertips and continue. Tears in the middle of the roll are invisible in the finished loaf. Only a tear at the seam end needs careful patching.

Do I really need a cloth to roll potica?

It is the traditional method for a reason: the cloth prevents sticking and lets you lift the dough into the roll without stretching it by hand. A well-floured silicone mat is an acceptable substitute; a bare counter is not.
