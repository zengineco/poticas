How to Make Povitica

By Poticas.com  ·  Updated August 2026

The Croatian cousin that Kansas City made famous. Povitica is stretched tablecloth-thin, packed with walnut, and baked low and slow into a dense, marbled loaf. Here is the full recipe.

How do you make povitica?

Make an enriched yeast dough of flour, milk, butter, eggs, and sugar, and let it rise. Stretch it paper-thin over a floured cloth until it covers a table, spread a warm ground-walnut filling edge to edge, roll it up, and coil it into a loaf pan. Bake low and slow — about 300°F for 15 minutes, then 250°F for another 45 to 60 — for the dense, many-layered slice povitica is known for.

The Kansas City Nut Roll

Povitica (say it poh-vuh-TEET-sah) is the Croatian branch of the same rolled-sweet-bread family as potica. In the United States it is most tied to Kansas City, where Croatian immigrants settled the Strawberry Hill neighborhood and where the Strawberry Hill Povitica Company later made the loaf nationally known.

It is the same idea as potica — thin dough, walnut filling, sliced to show a spiral — pushed to an extreme: the dough is stretched even thinner, the filling packed even heavier, and the loaf baked low and slow so the dense center cooks through. The payoff is that unmistakable marbled cross-section.

The Dough

An enriched yeast dough, soft and elastic enough to stretch without tearing:

  • 500 g bread flour
  • 1 packet (7 g) instant yeast
  • 240 ml warm milk
  • 80 g sugar
  • 2 eggs
  • 60 g butter, melted
  • ½ tsp salt

Mix and knead until smooth and stretchy, then cover and let it rise until doubled, about 1 to 1½ hours. A well-developed, elastic dough is what makes the later stretch possible.

The Walnut Filling

Povitica carries a lot of filling — mix it warm and spreadable:

  • 500 g walnuts, finely ground
  • 180 ml milk
  • 100 g butter
  • 150 g sugar
  • 1 egg
  • 1 tsp vanilla; 1 tbsp cocoa optional, for the darker classic look

Method: scald the milk and butter together, pour over the ground walnuts and sugar, then stir in the egg and vanilla to a thick, spreadable paste. Keep it warm and soft — a stiff, cold filling tears the thin dough.

The Stretch — the Defining Move

This is what separates povitica from an ordinary nut roll. Spread a clean cloth over a table and flour it. Roll the dough out with a pin as far as it will go, then slide your floured hands underneath and stretch it outward from the center until it is thin enough to see the cloth through — often as wide as the whole table.

Trim away the thick outer edge. Thin, even dough is the entire secret of the dense, many-layered povitica slice; roll it only with a pin and you will get a coarser loaf with fewer, fatter turns.

Roll and Bake Low and Slow

Spread the warm filling edge to edge over the stretched dough. Roll it up from a long side into a long rope, then coil that rope into a greased loaf pan — an S-shape or a spiral, folded to fit. Brush with egg wash and rest 15 minutes.

Bake low and slow: start at about 300 °F (150 °C) for 15 minutes, then drop to 250 °F (120 °C) for another 45 to 60 minutes, until the internal temperature reaches roughly 200 °F. The low heat is not optional — a dense, filling-heavy loaf at high heat scorches its thin, sugar-rich dough before the middle is cooked. Cool fully before slicing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between povitica and potica?

They are the same family. Povitica is the Croatian name, made famous in Kansas City; its dough is stretched even thinner and filled even heavier, then baked low and slow in a loaf pan for a dense, marbled slice. Potica is the Slovenian version and is often baked in a round fluted pan. See our potica vs povitica guide for the full comparison.

Why is povitica baked at such a low temperature?

Because the loaf is dense and thick with a wet walnut filling. Low, slow heat cooks the center through without scorching the thin, sugar-rich dough. High heat would brown the outside long before the middle set.

Can I make povitica without stretching it on a tablecloth?

You can roll it thin with a pin, but the signature dense, many-layered slice comes from the hand-stretch that takes the dough to near-translucent. Rolled-only loaves are still good, but they have fewer, thicker layers and a coarser spiral.

How long does povitica keep?

Well wrapped, several days at room temperature and up to a month frozen. The heavy walnut filling keeps it moist. See our guide on storing potica for wrapping and freezing details.
